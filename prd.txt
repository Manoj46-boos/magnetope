2.3 Why Games Get Rejected
Poki documents its rejection logic with unusual candor, and the pattern matters more than any single rule. Two
categories are rejected immediately: misused IP (anything that borrows recognizable characters, names, or
trade dress) and adult themes - graphic violence including open wounds and visible body fluids, sexual
content, sustained scary themes, gambling, alcohol, tobacco, bullying, stereotypes, and sexism. Poki is a
playground that includes children, and its advertising partners must be comfortable placing brands next to every
game. This is why the classic web physics-comedy lineage of Happy Wheels - which is built on gore - cannot
simply be ported to Poki, and why MAGNETOPE's fail states are designed as pure slapstick: tumbling,
bouncing, and comedic collapse, never injury.
Beyond the hard categories, the most common quality declines are buggy or broken gameplay, excessive
load size or poor performance, confusing UX, and poor or inconsistent graphics. A subtle but decisive
rule follows: games that feel too derivative may be declined even when they work perfectly, and when a genre
or trend becomes crowded, Poki actively declines additional similar submissions because overlapping games
fragment the audience and drag each other down. Saturated or high-risk categories as of October 2026 include
generic 2D platformers, .io arena clones, dress-up variants, merge and idle management, block puzzles,
stickman fighting, and endless runners.

2.4 The AI Production Bar
Poki's Working-with-AI guidance is the single most important document for this project, because the
developer's stated plan is to build with an AI subscription and personal craft skills. The golden rule reads: AI
should empower, not shortcut. Prototyping with templates, inspiration, and AI tools is normal; but before a
game enters player testing it must reflect the creator's unique perspective and show meaningful improvement
beyond the base tooling. The more non-original material a game leans on, the higher the risk it will not be
accepted - even if it functions well. Ownership matters too: the developer must be able to show how and which
tools were used, with prompts and iterations on request, because purely AI-created work can create IP
ownership problems.
The guidance also specifies a per-craft do-and-don't table that maps exactly onto this project's workflow: use
AI for concepts and then heavily edit into one cohesive direction; never drop raw output in as-is; never ship
untested generated levels - every level must be hand-balanced; use AI for code boilerplate while a human owns
testing and optimization; keep every visual watermark-free and prompt-text-free. MAGNETOPE
institutionalizes these rules as a documented asset pipeline in Chapters 6, 11, and 16, including a kept
prompt-and-iteration log that can be shown to Poki on request. Where the average AI-submitted game fails this
bar, this project treats the bar as a design constraint from day one.
Experimenting with templates, inspiration, and AI tools while prototyping is normal. But before

3.3 Player, Tone, and Comedy
The primary persona is the 13-18-year-old web gamer: plays in a browser tab on a school Chromebook or a
mid-tier Android phone, watches Poki-adjacent content on TikTok and YouTube, discovers games through
thumbnails and friends, and values skill expression and shareable fail moments above narrative depth. This
player does not read; this player drops in, dies, laughs, retries, and - if the game is good - sends the clip to a
friend. The secondary persona is the older casual who played Flash physics games in the 2010s and recognizes
the genre's pleasure instantly.
Tone is Saturday-morning-cartoon slapstick crossed with a Saturday-morning yard sale. The world is a
monument to abandoned objects - fridges, shopping carts, satellite dishes, gym lockers - stacked into
impossible geography. Humor comes from physics truth: a filing cabinet that wobbles before it topples; a
trampoline-styled mattress that launches you exactly one meter too far; a magnet-headed robot slowly sliding
down a refrigerator door with a rising squeak. There is no dialogue beyond environmental signage, no narrator,
and no gore: MAGNO is a robot, so every fail is a tumble, a bounce, and a spring-loaded pop back upright.

entry with a single unstructured mountain; it is not on Poki. Drive Mad - one of Poki's highest-rated games at
4.3 stars with 200+ levels - owns simple-controls-plus-rage in short structured levels, but is a driving game with
no climbing, no character comedy, and no physics-body humor. Only Up owns vertical junk-tower fantasy and
went violently viral on TikTok in 2023, but its web copies are low-effort clones confined to third-tier portals,

3.5 Narrative: Wordless, Environmental, Wholesome
Centuries ago, something magnetically special happened at one spot on Earth. Loose metal from the whole
world drifted there over generations and compacted into a mountain - the Magnetope. MAGNO, a small
scrap-golem with a horseshoe-magnet head and exactly one eye, lost its power core in an avalanche and wakes
at the mountain's base with one goal: climb to the peak and get its heart back. The story is told entirely through
environment: faded festival banners in the Scrapyard biome, a suburb's worth of lawn mowers and mailboxes in
Suburbia, neon signage in Metropolis, a giant rusted playground in the fourth biome, and finally the thin-air
silence of Stratosphere, where the core glows like a lighthouse. The ending is a sixty-second wordless victory:
the core clicks into MAGNO's chest, the magnet head's field lines bloom outward across the sky, and the
camera pulls back to reveal the whole absurd mountain one last time.
3.6 Naming Strategy
Working title: MAGNETOPE - a portmanteau of magnet and -ope (as in envelope, slope, and telescope) that
names the mountain itself. The word is unique: a search across Poki, CrazyGames, itch.io, and general web
search in October 2026 returns zero game-title collisions, which satisfies Poki's name-and-thumbnail
dimension (presentation focused on the game's own content, not riding search terms). It is short, pronounceable
in every launch locale, reads well in uppercase on a thumbnail, and leaves room for sequel naming

4 The Signature Mechanic: Polarity Flip
4.1 The Three Verbs
The entire control surface is three verbs. STICK: whenever any part of MAGNO's body contacts a
ferromagnetic surface while attract mode is active, a magnetic joint forms instantly and MAGNO adheres -
hanging, dangling, or rolling in place on walls and ceilings. FLIP: the single player-issued input reverses
polarity. If attached, the joint inverts into a repulsor, launching MAGNO away from the surface along its
contact normal with a force proportional to charge held; if airborne, flip arms the snap. SNAP: while airborne
in attract mode, the nearest ferromagnetic surface inside snap radius is highlighted, and releasing flip (or
pressing it again, configuration-dependent) pulls MAGNO toward it with a spring force that converts cleanly
into orbital momentum. Chaining flip-snap-flip turns the tower into a vertical slingshot course.
Momentum is sacred. The physics model deliberately never zeroes velocity on attach; instead, surface friction
bleeds it over 250 milliseconds, so a fast arrival can convert into a wall-crawl or a pendulum swing around a
girder's edge. This single choice - conservation over reset - is what makes the mechanic learnable in ten seconds
and still improving after ten hours, and it is the difference between this design and a teleporting grapple.

4.3 Material Rules
Every surface in the game declares exactly one material family, and the material grid is short enough to
memorize visually in one biome. Ferromagnetic surfaces (steel girders, fridges, cars, lockers, filing cabinets,
satellite dishes) participate in stick, flip, and snap at full strength. Weak-metal surfaces (aluminum gutters,
thin sheet metal, cans) hold stick for a degrading 1.2 seconds - long enough to plan, short enough to panic - and
are visually thinner and rustier. Non-metallic surfaces (wood, rubber, glass, plastic, fabric) never hold stick:
MAGNO bounces, slides, or rolls off, and each has its own restitution (mattress 0.75, rubber 0.65, wood 0.3,
glass 0.1 with a breakable flag). Live-ferrous props (springs, fans, elevator counterweights) are dynamic
bodies: MAGNO's field drags them, they drag back, and loose-junk chain reactions emerge from the
simulation rather than being scripted.

4.5 Anti-Frustration Grammar
Rage is the product, but unfairness is poison, so the mechanic obeys a strict fairness grammar. Falls never
damage; they only cost altitude and time. The camera pulls back automatically during long falls so the player
can read the landing zone, and a soft auto-align rotates MAGNO's snap cone toward the nearest safe hold
during the final 300 milliseconds of a fall - an assist that defaults on, is tunable, and can be disabled by expert
players for score-boost purposes. Every fall longer than twelve meters triggers the slow-mo system,
guaranteeing that even punishment feels like a produced moment. Retry state is captured the instant a run
starts, so restart rebuilds the level in under 400 milliseconds with zero loading screens.

5.1 The Core Loop
The loop is a closed six-step cycle engineered to complete in under three minutes and to leave the player
mid-loop when any interruption - ad, tab switch, bus arrival - occurs, which is the web-session survival strategy
Poki's engagement doctrine demands. Load dumps the player directly into gameplay; climbing is continuous
action with zero downtime; failing is content rather than punishment; retrying costs one tap and 400
milliseconds; scoring pays out three currencies of achievement (stars, speed, Scrap); and unlocking happens at

5.2 Level Anatomy
Every level is a vertical gauntlet of 30-90 seconds for a competent first clear, built from four repeating zones.
The base camp (first 5 seconds) is always safe: a wide platform, one obvious first stick-and-flip, zero threats -
this is where session-zero players learn the level's dialect. The twist introduces the level's one new idea (a fan
that pushes mid-flight, a crane hook that swings, weak-metal shelves that start shedding) with a guaranteed-safe
practice encounter. The gauntlet combines the twist with the biome's established vocabulary at rising intensity,
and is tuned to produce exactly two to four failures for a median player on first visit. The summit is the final
10 meters: always visible from the base, always marked by a glowing collection beacon, and always one
satisfying charged flip away. Length is a hard budget: levels that playtest longer than 90 seconds get split, no
exceptions, because the three-minute loop 容纳 two retries plus a clear.

5.4 Difficulty Architecture
Difficulty is specified numerically as a median fall rate per attempt - the percentage of level entries that end
in a fall rather than a clear - because it is directly measurable by the ML playtest bots of Chapter 11 before a
human ever touches the build. The target curve ramps from roughly 6% at level 1 to 68% at level 70, with each
biome's opening levels stepping down 5-8 points to grant a breather after the previous biome's climax. The rage
band - the range where failure feels funny rather than unfair - sits between 18% and 35% for mid-game levels;
above 40% the level is flagged for softening, and below 12% it is flagged as filler and gets a twist sharpened.
Spikes are deliberately built in: roughly one in five levels is a conflict level that runs 10-15 points above the
local trend, because those are the levels that become clips, TikTok captions, and schoolyard legend. The curve
below is the shipping contract; the bots enforce it per level and per biome before every content milestone.

5.5 Progression Systems
Three parallel progression tracks keep three kinds of player moving forward. Stars: every level awards up to
three - one for clearing, one for clearing without a fall beyond the safety line (the no-fall run), and one for
beating the par time shown on the level card. Stars gate biome unlocks at soft thresholds (biome 2 at 15 stars,
biome 3 at 34, biome 4 at 57, biome 5 at 80), so a player who struggles can still advance by clearing optional
challenges, while completionists have 210 stars to chase. Speedrun ghosts: every level's par time ships with a
built-in developer ghost; beating par unlocks a personal ghost, and Chapter 15's AUDS-based ghost exchange
makes friend ghosts and regional top-10 ghosts appear in-world. Scrap: the single meta currency, earned 10-50
per level on first clear and 2-8 on replays, spent purely on cosmetics (Chapter 13) so progression never gates
content behind grind - a deliberate Poki-doctrine choice that keeps the natural reward path free.
5.6 Fail States and the Comedy System
Failure is a designed content category, not an absence of success. Physically, every fall switches MAGNO to
limp ragdoll with exaggerated joint looseness; the body tumbles, bounces with material-appropriate boings,
drags loose junk into a small avalanche, and settles. The recovery system then plays a procedural get-up - a
spring-loaded pop upright that reads as dignified stubbornness - before control returns. A fall is scored: the
post-fall card shows fall height, bounces, junk displaced, and a letter grade from C to SSS, with the best falls of
a session collected into a rolling highlight strip on the level-select screen. This is the word-of-mouth engine in
miniature: the game literally grades your funniest failure, and the share-card system of Chapter 15 turns that
grade into a PNG the player can post. No fall anywhere in the game produces injury imagery, blood, or distress
- MAGNO is metal, the world is junk, and every collapse ends in a bounce.

6 Art Direction Domain
6.1 Style Bible
The visual language is stylized low-poly flat-shaded 3D with a toy-scale diorama feeling: models live between
200 and 900 triangles, every prop reads as a chunky collectible rather than a simulation, and the whole
mountain composes like a shelf of oversized knick-knacks. Flat shading (one normal per face, vertex colors
preferred over textures wherever a prop is a solid color) gives the game its two free superpowers: tiny file sizes
and instant silhouette readability at thumbnail scale. Detail lives where gameplay lives - a fridge door has a
visible handle seam because the handle is a grabbable edge - and nowhere else. Nothing in the game uses
photoreal textures, normal maps, or photographic source material, which keeps the art style defensibly original
and cheap to stream.
Three rules govern every asset review, and any model that fails one is reworked rather than excused. First, the
silhouette test: a flat black render of the prop must be identifiable to a playtester in under a second, because
players read shapes before colors and mobile screens before shapes. Second, the material truth test: the prop's
magnetic class must be guessable from geometry alone - steel looks dense and riveted, weak-metal looks thin
and crumpled, non-metal looks matte or soft. Third, the budget test: the model, compressed, must fit its slot in
the per-biome asset budget of Chapter 10, or it does not ship regardless of quality.
6.2 Color and Lighting System
Color is narrative: the mountain tells its story through a temperature journey from warm ground-level junk to
cool stratospheric silence, and every biome owns a dominant family with a single contrasting accent used only
on gameplay-relevant edges (grabbable ferromagnetic surfaces carry a faint cyan rim tint by default, adjustable
in accessibility settings). Lighting is one directional sun with a fixed per-biome angle and a warm-to-cool color
story, plus a hemisphere fill; there are no dynamic shadows on mobile tier - a baked vertex AO pass at import
time provides depth for near-zero cost. Distance fog is per-biome and doubles as both mood and a
draw-distance curtain: rust haze in the Scrapyard, laundry-soft white in Suburbia, blue dusk fog in Metropolis,
chalky glare in the Playground, and near-vacuum star-clear air in the Stratosphere.

6.3 Character Design: MAGNO
MAGNO is engineered as an animation rig first and a mascot second. The body is a rounded capsule (roughly
0.6 m tall, 0.45 m diameter) whose round bottom guarantees that every fall ends in a roll or a wobble instead of
a crunch - slapstick is baked into the skeleton. The head is an oversized horseshoe magnet with chrome-red
tips, tilted at a permanent 8-degree confident angle; the face is a single large eye in a dark socket that handles
the entire expression system (squash for determination, wide for falling, half-lid for smug). Arms are
two-segment noodles with spherical hands that grab automatically during stick. Total triangle budget: 480 for
body + head, 120 per arm. The silhouette is a squat bowling-pin with a U on top - identifiable at 32 pixels,
which is exactly the size the thumbnail demands. Skins recolor the chrome ring, the eye socket, and add small
clip-on props (antenna, sticker slap, tiny flag); no skin ever changes silhouette, preserving gameplay readability
and zero-cost thumbnail consistency.
6.4 Environment and Prop Language
Every biome's prop library is authored as a family of 12-15 master props with modular variants, targeting 60
unique physics identities across launch (Table 5.2). Design language rules keep the world coherent: all junk
shares a scale story (objects are 1.2-2× real-world scale so MAGNO always looks like a toy among toys), all
metal carries rivets or seams, all weak-metal carries dents, and all interactive surfaces exaggerate their
affordance (a grabbable handle is 1.4× normal size). The 20 dynamic props are the comedy engine: girders that
see-saw, washing machines that boing, filing cabinets that shed drawers, playground swings that act as
pendulums, a ferris wheel that turns on MAGNO's field. Each prop's physical parameters (mass, friction,
restitution, magnetic class) live in one data table that both the artists' Blender kit and the runtime consume, so
visual identity and physics identity can never drift apart.

7 Animation Domain
7.1 Philosophy: Physics Is the Animator
In a ragdoll physics game, the simulation supplies locomotion and the animation layer supplies intent and
personality. MAGNO's motion is a three-layer system: the physics body does the falling, the procedural
animation layer does the reacting, and a small library of hand-authored poses stamps readable emotion on top.
The craft goal is that a spectator watching a silent clip can tell the difference between a player's panicked flail
and a confident ascent purely from secondary motion - arms trailing on fast flips, windmilling during long falls,
a smug idle drum of the fingers on level clear. Every animation decision serves either readability (what is this
body doing) or charm (who is this body), and anything serving neither is cut.
7.2 Squash and Stretch Contract
Squash and stretch is the style's heartbeat, and its amounts are specified numerically so they stay consistent
across thousands of procedural contexts. Impact squash on attach: scale to 85% along the contact normal for 90
ms with a 140 ms elastic recovery. Release stretch on flip: 118-124% along the impulse vector, proportional to
charge power, recovering over the first 200 ms of flight. Long-fall stretch: a gentle 108% vertical while in fast
free-fall, sold further by the eye narrowing. Landing-from-height squash bottoms at 72% with a dust ring
scaled to impact speed. The magnet head participates inversely (stretch when the body squashes) so total
volume reads as preserved - the classic principle applied at rig level rather than keyframe level, since almost no

MAGNETOPE — Product Requirements Document
Magnetope Studio
19
animation in this game is keyframed.
7.3 The Ragdoll Blend System
MAGNO exists on a continuous spectrum between four body states, blended by weights rather than switched.
Active (full motor control, spring forces hold the capsule upright and arms track targets), Scramble (a hit of
chaos: control authority dips to 40% for 400 ms after hard contacts - the wobble that reads as being rattled),
Limp (all motor forces off, pure ragdoll, engaged for falls and big impacts), and Recover (a 650 ms
spring-powered get-up sequence that is procedurally initiated from whatever pose the body settled in, using
torque-to-upright plus a two-beat arm push-off, ending in a 120 ms settle bounce). The get-up is the most
important animation in the game - it plays after every fail - so it is authored as a parameterized system (spring
constants ramp, head tilt correction, eye re-focus) rather than a canned clip, ensuring it never repeats identically
and never clips through geometry because it is computed from the actual resting pose.
7.4 IK Arms and Secondary Motion
Arms are two-bone IK chains driven by target points that lag the body's motion by 80 ms, which is the entire
secret of weight: fast direction changes make the arms trail, stops make them overshoot, and the character
instantly feels massy. Hand targets have three behaviors - grab-seek (during stick approach, hands reach toward
the surface within a 30° cone), free-float (a damped noise orbit around the shoulder line during flight,
amplitude scaled by airspeed), and brace (pre-impact anticipation pose 150 ms before predicted landing). The
head runs its own smaller lag chain (30 ms) with magnetic orientation bias toward the current target surface, so
the eye naturally leads the player's intent - a subtle, constant readability aid.

8.3 The Slow-Motion Fall System
Big falls are the game's signature shareable moment, so they are produced like a shot in a film rather than left
to chance. Trigger: fall height above 12 meters or impact speed above 14 m/s. On trigger, time scale ramps to
0.35 over 150 ms, the camera dollies back and up to frame the arc, a radial speed-line overlay fades in at 8%
opacity, the falling whistle drops in pitch with the timescale (audio and simulation share one clock), and field
lines trail the body in limp mode. On impact, a single frame of white flash at 5% opacity, dust ring, time scale
recovers over 450 ms, and the fall-grade card slides in. The system has a per-session budget (maximum 4
slow-mo falls per 3 minutes, prioritizing the biggest) so the effect never becomes noise, and it is fully disabled
by the reduced-motion accessibility setting, which substitutes a fixed wide camera with no time distortion.
8.4 Screen Shake Grammar
Shake is a language with a strict grammar, not a volume knob. Micro-shake (amplitude 0.10 of camera height,
80 ms): stick attach on weak metal, prop collisions nearby. Small (0.22, 120 ms): charged flip release, dynamic
prop topple. Big (0.38, 180 ms): high-speed impact, avalanche events. Shake is positional noise in the 8-12 Hz
band with rotational component capped at 1.2 degrees, exponential decay, and a hard rule that it never triggers
during snap-in-progress or any input-critical window, because ruining aim is the cheapest way to make a
physics game feel unfair. The reduced-motion setting reduces all amplitudes by 70% and removes rotational
shake entirely.
8.5 Post-Processing and Mobile Fallback
The desktop post stack is deliberately short: a soft bloom (threshold 0.85, strength 0.4 - used only on beacons,
the core, and field lines), a 12% vignette, and a per-biome grading LUT authored in Blender's film color tools
and baked to a 256×16 texture. The mobile tier drops bloom to half-resolution or disables it (a quality flag
decides at device-detection time), keeps the LUT (one texture fetch, effectively free), and replaces the vignette
with a pre-baked corner darkening in the biome's gradient fog. No depth-of-field, no motion blur, no chromatic
aberration: each was tested and cut - the first two cost frames on low-end devices, and aberration reads as
rendering error at small scale rather than style.

9 Audio Domain
9.1 Philosophy and the Anti-Slop Moat
The audio direction exists to make MAGNETOPE sonically undeniable as a handcrafted product in a market
flooded with stock-loop games. Every surface has a material-coded voice; every system state has a continuous
sonic signature; the music is a set of adaptive stems that respond to altitude and momentum; and the total
shipped audio footprint is under 350 KB because nearly everything is synthesized at runtime from oscillators,
noise, and filters rather than shipped as recordings. This is not an austerity measure - it is the sound of the
game's identity: a world built from junk should sound assembled from raw elements, precisely tuned. It also
happens to be the single strongest answer to "this looks like every AI game": no other submission will sound
like this one.

9.3 The Polarity Hum System
The game's most distinctive continuous sound is the magnet itself. In attract mode, a low hum of 55 Hz + 110
Hz partials swells with proximity to ferromagnetic mass - the player literally hears how close the next hold is,
which doubles as a soft assist and a tension device while dangling. In repel mode the hum crossfades to a
detuned sawtooth shimmer whose brightness tracks charge level. When charge is full, a barely-perceptible 16
Hz tremolo makes the held flip feel physically tense. The hum is mixed at -30 LUFS-relative under normal play
so it colors rather than narrates, and the accessibility options offer a visual charge meter replacement for
hearing-impaired players.
9.4 Adaptive Music Architecture
Each biome ships four stems - ground (bass/percussion loop), layer 1 (melodic motif), layer 2 (harmonic
pad), and event (stingers: clear, big-fall, unlock) - authored in one key per biome so any layer combination is
consonant. The music director follows a deterministic rule set: ground always plays during a run; layer 1 fades
in above 40% of level height; layer 2 engages during snap-chain streaks (three surfaces in under six seconds);
during slow-mo, all stems low-pass and stretch with the timescale; on clear, stingers resolve to the biome's
tonic. Transitions are 400 ms equal-power crossfades, never instant cuts. The whole score is delivered as
Opus-encoded stems (48 kHz mono, ~40 KB per stem), decoded at load - approximately 800 KB across all five
biomes, streamed in the background with the biome chunks rather than in the initial payload.
9.5 Ambience and Wind
Ambience is one filtered-noise wind voice whose character evolves with altitude: Scrapyard ground level is
barely audible warm air with distant clanks (randomized metal creak events triggered by physics contacts far
below), Suburbia adds synthetic birdsong blips, Metropolis layers a hum of neon buzz, and Stratosphere strips
everything to a thin whistle and eventual silence - the quiet itself becomes the biome's identity. Gusts are shared
with the gameplay wind system, so audio wind and physical wind are one parameter, and the player can hear a
gust coming before it pushes them, which is both fair play and mood.

The game is structured as an ECS-lite: component stores (plain typed arrays and maps), systems that run in a
fixed order, and zero inheritance hierarchies in the hot path. The game loop is a fixed-timestep design -
simulation at exactly 60 Hz with an accumulator, rendering decoupled with interpolation of visual transforms -
because deterministic physics is a correctness requirement, not a nicety: the ML playtest bots of Chapter 11,
the speedrun ghosts, and the fail-replay capture all depend on the same simulation stepping identically given the
same inputs. The render side is one WebGL2 renderer instance with a scene graph per biome; a WebGPU path
sits behind a runtime flag for future investigation but WebGL2 is the shipping baseline since WebGPU is only
now reaching default-on status across Chrome, Edge, and Firefox and is still rolling out in Safari.
10.3 Build Budget and Progressive Loading
The byte budget is treated as a design constraint with the same authority as the difficulty curve. The initial
payload - everything needed to reach gameplay and clear the first three levels - totals 3.85 MB against the 4.0
MB internal target and the 5.0 MB Poki hard limit. Biome chunks (meshes, level data, music stems, locale files
beyond EN) stream in the background after session start, exactly the progressive-loading pattern Poki calls "one
of the most important techniques for success on the web"; the player is already playing while biome 2 arrives.
Total shipped size across all chunks is engineered to stay at or under 7 MB against the 8 MB ceiling. The
budget is enforced mechanically: the asset cook pipeline and the CI build both fail if any chunk exceeds its
allocation, so size can only regress through a deliberate decision, never through drift.

10.6 Scaling, Storage, and CSP Compliance
Rendering scales to Poki's required canonical sizes (640×360, 836×470, 1031×580) by letterboxing the fixed

in-memory store, continues fully playable, and surfaces a single soft toast that progress will not persist - exactly
the pattern Poki's requirements demand. The build performs zero external network requests at runtime: fonts
are a bundled subset, all assets ship in the bundle, and the only optional network surface (ghost exchange) uses
Poki's own AUDS platform API, which is first-party and pre-approved rather than an external service requiring
CSP exception review.

11 Machine Learning Domain
11.1 Policy: Intelligence Off the Wire
The ML policy is stated as an architecture constraint before it is a capability list: no model ships in the game
bundle. A WebGPU/WASM inference runtime alone would consume 2-8 MB of the 8 MB total budget and
add first-load latency, which would trade the game's two hardest-won platform advantages (size and speed) for
features the player cannot see. Every ML component therefore runs on the development machine, against the
deterministic fixed-timestep simulation, and its outputs ship as data: difficulty curves, level flags,
telemetry-derived thresholds, and balance patches. The player-facing game stays a hand-tuned 3.85 MB
artifact; the intelligence lives in the pipeline that produced it.
11.2 Playtest Bots
The flagship ML tool is a reinforcement-learning playtester. A PPO agent trains in a headless build of the real
game - same Rapier physics, same magnet controller, rendered nothing - on a curriculum of individual level
segments, receiving reward for altitude gained, level completion, and (weighted negatively) fall count and
wall-clock time. Training uses domain randomization over input jitter and reaction latency so the resulting
policy approximates a sloppy human rather than a frame-perfect robot. Each trained policy then plays every
level 1,000 times overnight, and the harness reports per-level: completability (any policy solves it), median
attempts-to-clear, fall-rate distribution, and the exact 3D positions where falls cluster. Levels that no policy
completes are broken by construction; levels whose fall rate drifts outside the curve contract of Figure 5.2 are
flagged into the designer's review queue. This closes the loop that makes the difficulty chapter enforceable
before a human ever playtests.
11.3 Telemetry and Difficulty Analytics
The shipped game emits a compact anonymous event stream - level entered, stick/flip/snap counts with
positions, fall events with height and landing cell, clear times, retry latency, session length - batched in memory
and flushed to the Poki dashboard's event system on natural stops (never mid-run, never during ads, and only
via first-party SDK channels so the CSP story stays clean). Development-side tooling clusters fail positions per
level (k-means over fall coordinates, k selected by silhouette score), renders them as heatmaps onto the level's
top-down layout, and mines retry sequences to find the specific obstacle where players quit rather than retry.
Weekly, the heatmap review answers three questions per level: where do players fail (is it the designed twist), is
the fail readable (do they understand why), and does the fail rate sit inside the contract band. The same
pipeline, pointed at soft-release data, becomes the live difficulty-tuning engine described in Chapter 15.

12 UX & UI Domain
12.1 Onboarding Contract
Poki's easy-access guidance - skip the menu, start safe, teach visually, introduce gradually - is implemented as a
second-by-second contract rather than a philosophy statement. The game boots directly into the Scrapyard base

reward offers), and the cosmetic locker. Every surface is fully navigable by keyboard, mouse, and touch with
the same focus order, satisfying Poki's input guidance (WASD/arrows through menus, space/enter as confirm).

14.3 Protocols
 • Performance protocol: nightly automated matrix run capturing frame-time histograms, physics-step p99,
memory after 30-minute soak (leak detection), and payload size; regressions fail the build.
• Event-trace protocol: every CI build replays 10,000 scripted inputs through the real input layer and asserts
the SDK invariants (start-on-first-input, no double-fires, no events during ad windows).
• Silent-mode protocol: full playthrough with audio muted - the game must remain fully completable with
zero audio-gated information (Chapter 9).
• Playtest protocol: before submission, 5 external playtesters (friends, family, then Poki Playtesting's real
players) with session recordings; review focuses on the first 90 seconds and the first fall.
• Bug triage: S0 blocks release (crash, broken level, SDK misfire); S1 fixes within 48 h (progression blocker,
perf below floor on reference tier); S2 next content update; S3 backlog.

15.3 The Word-of-Mouth Engine
Virality is designed, not hoped for. The game's clip-ability rests on three systems. Auto-captured fail replays:
the deterministic fixed-timestep simulation plus the input buffer means the last 8 seconds of any run can be
re-simulated exactly; the game renders the best fall of a session as a ready-made 9:16 share card (PNG via
canvas, saved locally at the player's touch - no external URLs, no uploads, fully CSP-safe) showing the fall
grade, height, and bounce count over a clean biome backdrop. TikTok-native readability: slow-mo falls,
polarity-colored field lines, and the fall-grade card are all legible at thumbnail scale with the sound off, which
is how clip content actually gets consumed. Ghost culture: friend and regional ghosts (via Poki's AUDS data
store - a first-party, pre-approved platform feature) turn every level into a leaderboard-shaped social object;
beating a friend's ghost produces its own share card. The strategy is one compounding loop: a funny fail gets
shared, a shared card carries the game's name and visual identity, a new player arrives and generates their own
fail - the same flywheel that made Drive Mad and Level Devil TikTok phenomena, but with the shareable
artifact manufactured by the game itself.

16.2 The Solo + AI Workflow
The weekly rhythm is deliberately craft-forward: Monday and Tuesday are content days (level grey-boxing,
prop modeling, hand-balancing), Wednesday is systems day (code, physics tuning, tests), Thursday is juice day
(animation, VFX, audio - the differentiators), Friday is verify day (bot sweeps, heatmap review, device matrix,
and a 30-minute self-playtest with recording). The AI subscription participates as a pair-programmer and
drafting partner under the Chapter 11.4 ledger: code review and refactoring suggestions, test scaffolding,
GLSL drafts, localization first passes, and documentation structure. What it never does is ship: every asset,
level, and tuning decision passes through the developer's hands, both because Poki's originality bar demands it
and because feel - the entire product - is not delegable.
16.3 Zero-Budget Resource Map
 • Engine, physics, audio: Three.js, Rapier, Web Audio API - all free and open-source.

telemetry.
• Cash cost of the entire production: the existing Claude subscription and electricity.

17.2 Decision Rules
Numbers exist to make decisions mechanical, and three rules govern the live game. If a level's live fall rate
drifts outside its contract band by more than 8 points for three days, it is patched in the next update window -
the data outranks the designer's affection. If soft-release C2P misses by more than 15 points after the loading
audit and thumbnail A/B, the problem is the first 60 seconds, and the intervention is onboarding surgery, never
feature addition. And if the game passes soft release but engagement holds flat through two update waves, the
year-one roadmap's fourth slot converts from content to a Chute-Mode early pull - because on a curated
platform, the graveyard is full of games that stopped being interesting, and none of them failed their QA
checklist.
